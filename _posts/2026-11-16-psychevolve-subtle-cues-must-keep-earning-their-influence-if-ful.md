  abstract: >-
    Full-duplex conversational agents can now see and hear users while speaking
    with them. Through video avatars, 3D avatars, or robots, they increasingly
    respond with nonverbal behavior as well as speech. This makes them promising
    partners for psychological support, where what matters is often what a
    person shows but does not say. Human listeners miss fleeting cues and forget
    much of what they notice. An agent could in principle attend to every frame,
    relate each cue to its own behavior, and remember what it learns about a
    person, and so reason psychologically and evolve. Current systems are still
    far from this, and we expect that better perception alone will not close the
    gap. Subtle cues are ambiguous, the agent changes what it observes, and
    memory turns passing readings into traits. We argue that if full-duplex
    agents are to reason psychologically and evolve, subtle cues must keep
    earning their influence. Following Brunswik's lens model, an agent should
    rely on a cue only as much as the cue has proved to reveal this person's
    appraisals, should not count its own influence as confirmation, and should
    trust the cue less when later evidence disagrees. We support this position
    by tracing how conversational agents have evolved toward full duplex,
    together with a brief bibliometric analysis of the field. To make the
    position concrete, we sketch PsychEvolve, a blueprint spanning data,
    architecture, and psychological grounding that raises open questions rather
    than claiming solutions, and we illustrate it with the design of an
    expert-in-the-loop avatar platform for psychological support.
